GPS Disciplined Oscillator

While there are plenty of open-source GPS-Disciplined Oscillator (GPSDO) designs out there, I decided to build my own to integrate a few specific features and performance improvements. At its core, the project uses a GNSS receiver to discipline a 10 MHz Oven-Controlled Crystal Oscillator (OCXO) for exceptional long-term frequency stability. Rather than relying on a raw timing pulse directly from the GPS module, the unit generates its own Pulse-Per-Second (PPS) output derived directly from the clean 10 MHz OCXO signal, phase-aligned to the GNSS time scale via an internal divider.

To feed multiple pieces of test gear without introducing ground loops, the unit features three galvanically isolated 50 Ω pure sine wave outputs for the 10 MHz reference clock. For timing applications, it also provides a 5 V TTL-compatible 50 Ω PPS output. An integrated 3.3 V bias tee on the RF input allows direct compatibility with standard, low-cost active GPS antennas, and the entire system is designed for autonomous, 24/7 continuous operation powered by a 12 V DC supply.

System Requirements

To keep the project focused, establish clear goals, and prevent scope creep, I am outlining a set of core requirements:

IDCategoryRequirement
REQ-01InterfaceThe system shall provide three galvanically isolated 10 MHz pure sine wave outputs.
REQ-02InterfaceEach 10 MHz output shall have a 50 Ω source impedance and a level of +10 dBm ±0.5 dB into a 50 Ω load.
REQ-03InterfaceThe system shall provide a 1 PPS output that is 5 V TTL compatible with a 50 Ω source impedance.
REQ-04InterfaceThe system shall be able to power an active GNSS antenna by providing a 3.3 V supply at the antenna input.
REQ-05InterfaceThe system shall operate from a 12 V DC external power supply.
REQ-06FunctionThe system shall derive the 1 PPS signal from the internal 10 MHz OCXO and align it to the GNSS time scale.
REQ-07FunctionThe system shall run continuously and autonomously, and shall recover without user intervention after loss of power or of the GNSS signal.
REQ-08PerformanceWhen locked, the 10 MHz output frequency shall be accurate to better than 1 × 10−12 averaged over 24 hours.
REQ-09PerformanceWhen locked, the Allan deviation (ADEV) of the 10 MHz output shall be less than 5 × 10−12 at τ = 1 s and less than 1 × 10−12 at τ = 10 000 s.
REQ-10PerformanceThe single-sideband phase noise of the 10 MHz output shall be better than −115 dBc/Hz at 10 Hz offset, −135 dBc/Hz at 100 Hz offset and −145 dBc/Hz at 1 kHz offset.
REQ-11PerformanceThe harmonics of the 10 MHz output shall be below −35 dBc and non-harmonic spurious signals below −70 dBc.
REQ-12PerformanceThe isolation between any two 10 MHz outputs shall be better than 70 dB.
REQ-13PerformanceThe 1 PPS output shall have a 10 to 90 % rise time and a 90 to 10 % fall time of less than 1 ns into a 50 Ω load.
REQ-14PerformanceWhen locked, the 1 PPS edge shall be aligned to the GNSS time scale to within ±50 ns.
REQ-15PerformanceThe system shall reach the locked state within 15 minutes of power-on with a valid GNSS signal.
REQ-16PerformanceDuring loss of the GNSS signal, the 10 MHz output shall stay within ±1 × 10−9 of its nominal frequency for at least 24 hours (holdover).
REQ-17MechanicalThe GNSS antenna input shall use an SMA connector.
REQ-18MechanicalEach 10 MHz output shall use a BNC connector.
REQ-19MechanicalThe 1 PPS output shall use a BNC connector.
REQ-20MechanicalThe system shall fit inside an aluminium extrusion enclosure.

Conceptual Block Diagram

Below is a high-level block diagram of the GPSDO system. The GNSS module receives signals from the GPS satellites and provides timing information to the FPGA, which implements a control loop to discipline the OCXO. The disciplined 10 MHz signal is then amplified and filtered before being sent to the isolated outputs. The DAC and amplifier are used to generate the PPS signal, which is also isolated before being output.

Power and connections The unit runs from a 12 V DC jack. A switching regulator (SMPS) does the heavy lifting of converting that down efficiently, and a linear regulator follows it to give a quiet supply for the sensitive analogue and timing circuits. There are two other inputs: an SMA connector for the GPS antenna, which also supplies the antenna's 3.3 V bias, and a USB-C port, which connects to a USB Blaster for programming and debugging the FPGA. The control loop The FPGA is the brain of the system. It takes the 10 MHz from the OCXO and divides it down to produce its own 1 pulse-per-second (PPS) signal. The GNSS module produces a 1 PPS of its own, locked to GPS time. If the OCXO were running at exactly 10 MHz, the two pulses would line up perfectly. In practice they never do, and the difference between them is exactly the information the loop needs. A Time-to-Digital Converter (TDC) measures that difference with very fine resolution, far finer than the FPGA could manage by counting clock cycles alone. It reports the measured interval to the FPGA over SPI. The FPGA works out how far the local pulse has drifted and sends a correction to a precision DAC. The DAC produces a control voltage, called the EFC (electronic frequency control) voltage, which gently pulls the OCXO's frequency back towards the right value. This repeats continuously. Each second the measurement is taken, the correction is calculated, and the OCXO is steered a tiny amount. The FPGA also talks to the GNSS module over UART for configuration and status. The outputs The OCXO's 10 MHz signal goes through an amplifier that feeds three identical output channels. Each channel has a driver and filter stage to produce a clean sine wave, followed by galvanic isolation, then a BNC connector. The isolation matters when you connect several instruments: it breaks the ground path between them, which avoids ground loops and the hum and noise they can cause. The fourth output is the 1 PPS signal, generated by the FPGA from the OCXO rather than taken straight from the GPS module. It passes through a TTL driver and a series resistor with ESD protection before reaching its BNC connector. Because the 10 MHz outputs and the 1 PPS come from the same disciplined oscillator, they stay phase-aligned to the GNSS time scale.
